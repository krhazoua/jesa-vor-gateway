           3.                  Process Description

           3.1                 Chemistry of the Process

           3.1.1               Overview

           The phosphoric acid plant process will consist of one (1) reaction train and two (2) filtration trains. The Concentration
A03
           and Fluorine Recovery system will consist of four (4) trains in parallel. The process described above is di-hydrate process.

           For Acid clarifiers, Four (4) settlers will be designed (two (2) for weak Phosphoric Acid, two (2) for Concentrated
           Phosphoric Acid) for sludge elimination.

           3.1.2               Key to successful operation

           The key to successful operation of the phosphoric acid plant is the control and operation of the chemical reaction
           occurring in the attack system. The objectives in controlling the attack reaction are:

                 •      To obtain a maximum extraction of P2O5 from the rock.
                 •      To provide an easily filtrable and washable gypsum so there will be a minimal amount of soluble P2O5 discharged
                        with the waste gypsum.

           The recipe for producing phosphoric acid is to produce good gypsum. When the gypsum produced is low in P2O5, easily
           filtered and easily washed, then the production rate and efficiency of the phosphoric acid plant are maximised.

           3.1.3               Chemical Reactions

           The principal reaction occurring in phosphoric acid production is the reaction between tricalcium phosphate and sulfuric
           acid to give soluble phosphoric acid and insoluble calcium sulfate dihydrate (gypsum).


                              (1)     Ca3(PO4)2 + 3 H2SO4 + 6 H2O                       3 CaSO4,2H2O + 2 H3PO4

           This reaction takes place in two steps, Phosphoric acid first dissolves the rock in the digestion reaction to form a solution
           of monocalcium phosphate as shown in equation 1a. The monocalcium phosphate then reacts with sulfuric acid to form
           phosphoric acid and gypsum as shown in equation 1b.

                              (1a)    6 H2O + Ca3(PO4)2 + 4 H3PO4                           3 Ca(H2PO4)2,2H2O

                              (1b)    3 Ca(H2PO4)2, 2H2O + 3 H2SO4                      3 CaSO4,2H2O + 6 H3PO4

           In addition to this major reaction, there is a number of minor reactions occurring. The most important are:

     The sodium and potassium fluosilicates (Last reaction) are a source of considerable trouble as the phosphoric acid will
     be saturated with them. These fluosilicates have a steep solubility versus temperature curve. Fluosilicates form scale in
     pipelines and equipment as the acid cools.

     Based on experience, the sulfate concentration in the attack system is normally to be maintained at about 2% excess
     expressed as SO3.

     It is important to closely control the sulfate content. Too little excess sulfate can increase P2O5 loss by causing dicalcium
     phosphate to co-precipitate with gypsum, leading to co-crystallized P2O5 losses. On the other hand, too much free
     sulfate can cause gypsum to coat the rock particles, resulting in incomplete reactions and unreacted P2O5 losses. Both
     situations lead to P2O5 losses. Additionally, very high sulfate levels may create an unstable form of calcium sulfate, which
     is difficult to filter and may clog the filter.

     Iron and aluminium form phosphate compounds (FePO4 & AIPO4) which usually remain in the hot solution exiting the
     attack and filtration process. Subsequent ageing and cooling will precipitate these compounds as sludge. Aluminium and
     Iron can precipitate in other phosphate forms at higher P2O5 concentration. Ageing and cooling of the concentrate acid
     will precipitate these compounds as sludge. However, this sludge is citrate soluble and its P2O5 content is available in the
     fertilizer.

     HF and SiF4 are generated partly from the attack tanks and in the flash cooler, and mainly in the evaporation step. Fluorine
     recovery takes place in the gas scrubber and evaporator scrubbers

     P2O5 losses will come from extraction losses occurring in the attack system and water-soluble losses occurring in the
     filtration section. There are two types of extraction losses:

     insufficient grinding or insufficient contacting of the phosphate rock with the sulfuric acid. This loss may also be due to
     the coating of the rock particles with a gypsum layer which occurs when the free sulfate content in the liquid phase of

     phosphoric acid do not have time to diffuse away from the rock/acid interface before being converted to calcium sulfate.
     The point at which this effect becomes serious varies with the type of rock, the concentration of acid and other
     conditions. It usually occurs when the free SO3 concentration exceeds about 3.5% in the liquid phase of the attack slurry.


                  The second type of extraction loss is the co-crystallised P2O5 and is also called citrate soluble P2O5 loss. This loss
     is due to the precipitation of dicalcium phosphate (CaHPO4, 2H2O) in the gypsum crystals. As the gypsum crystallises, it
     does not do so as pure calcium sulfate. A certain percentage of HPO42- (phosphate ions) substitutes for SO42- (sulfate ions)
     in the crystal lattice. Dicalcium phosphate crystallises in the same way as calcium sulfate and has nearly the same
     crystallisation characteristics. This loss is greatest in solutions having a high calcium oxide (CaO) concentration and a low
     sulfate concentration.


     To minimise citrate soluble losses, a slight but definite excess of sulfate ions (SO42-) must be maintained through the
     attack and filtration system.

     Filtration considerations make it desirable to produce gypsum, which is the most stable form of calcium sulfate.
     Hemihydrate in the presence of water or dilute phosphoric acid will tend to hydrate to gypsum, and this hydration can
     result in the cementing of gypsum crystals in the filter system.

     The equilibrium plot between the three forms of calcium sulfate as a function of P2O5 concentration, sulfate excess and
     temperature shows that at temperatures below 62°C up to 40% P2O5 can be maintained without production of
     hemihydrate. This equilibrium is also dependent upon the impurities in the acid and therefore varies with the type and
     grade of rock.

     As the acid strength increases, the temperature must be decreased to produce stable gypsum crystals. Generally, above
     32% P2O5 acid, the crystals rapidly diminish in size, filtration capacity is greatly reduced and washing of the cake becomes
     more difficult. Operation with the temperatures below 70°C will generally reduce filtration rates.

     For these reasons, a 26% to 30% P2O5 acid with a corresponding temperature of 80°C to 71°C is normally found to exist
     in conventional wet dihydrate process acid plants. In this specific project, a 28% P2O5 acid is targeted with a temperature
     of 76°C.


     3.1.6            Crystallization

     Crystallisation or precipitation is induced through supersaturation. Supersaturation occurs when either CaO or H2SO4 is
     added to a reaction system which is already saturated with these compounds.

     When H2SO4 is added to a phosphoric acid slurry containing CaO, the concentration of H2SO4 instantaneously rises and
     then drops as CaSO4 precipitates. The total H2SO4 decreases by an amount proportional to the decrease in the dissolved
     CaO.

     Excessive supersaturation occurs when the rate of crystallisation is not fast enough to remove the added CaO or H2SO4
     from solution. Excessive supersaturation can cause equipment problems and is therefore undesirable; precipitation may
     occur at places where it is not wanted.

     Supersaturation is kept at a minimum by maintaining a very large circulation of slurry to provide the surface for the
     crystallisation. Supersaturation is also held to a minimum by maintaining an excess of sulfuric acid at all points in the
     system so that at no time there is an excessive concentration of unprecipitated Ca++ (calcium ions). Mixing of the sulfuric
     acid with the recycle acid in the mixing tees helps in maintaining a uniform excess sulfuric acid concentration throughout
     the attack system.

     The agitation in the attack tank is also a very important factor. Good agitation must achieve the following objectives:

     Precipitation of gypsum from a solution containing excess calcium (low sulfates) yields small needle like crystals. The
     crystals become smaller and thinner as the concentration of Ca2+ in solution is increased. Decreasing the Ca2+ and
     increasing the SO42- concentration in the solution causes the crystals to grow in width and size. They are then easier to
     filter.


     3.1.7             Flash Cooler Temperature Drop

     It is desirable to minimise the number of crystals per unit weight of gypsum precipitated, the fewer the crystals, the
     larger their average size. Larger crystals are easier to filter.

     Supersaturation with respect to impurities provides the proper conditions for extensive nucleation.

     Impurity supersaturation can occur in the slurry when it is cooled by evaporation in the vacuum flash cooler. To minimise
     impurity supersaturation (and consequent over-nucleation), the slurry temperature drop across the flash cooler should
     be limited to below 5°C. A design of 2.5°C is usually selected.


     3.2              Attack and Digestion Sections

     This section is intended to produce phosphoric acid at a concentration of 28% P2O5

     3.2.1             Attack Section

     The attack section takes place in the attack reactor which consists of a monolithic concrete having multiple agitated
     compartments. The large downwards pumping ensures good suspension of solids and prevents depositions on the tank
     floor.

     All compartments of the attack reactor are interconnected in the upper part (gas phase) which are connected to the gas
     scrubbing system. This will help maintaining the reactor under a slight depression avoiding the escape of evolved gases
     and assist to some extent in the cooling of the slurry.

     The phosphate rock is fed to the reactor under the form of a 65% solids slurry (TBC). To maintain the capacity of the plant
     constant, the solid flowrate must be monitored. This is performed using a flowmeter and a densimeter.

     It is extremely important to have an accurate flow rate control of the phosphate rock feed and the sulfuric acid feed to
     regulate the free sulfate concentration in the reaction slurry. The concentrated sulfuric acid feed, rather than the rock

     not change the plant capacity with respect to P2O5 production.

     The sulfate concentration measured by manual sampling and analysis, is normally maintained between 25 and 30 g/l of

     The sulfuric acid, which is received from OSBL, is pre-mixed before entry into the Attack reactor with the recycled acid

     the return acid and the density by the cake wash water flow.

     Control of the recycled acid stream, and thus the P2O5, and solids level in the attack system is crucial. High P2O5 levels
     could result in crystallisation and viscosity problems which will limit filtration rate. Low solids contents may could increase
     foam formation and lead to supersaturation in the slurry. High solids contents increase slurry viscosity and cause mainly
     mixing and pumping issues.

     The chemical reactions occurring in the Attack Reactor are exothermic and along with the heat of dilution of the added
     sulfuric acid it means that in order to maintain the reaction temperature at the desired value of 76 °C to ensure the
     production of dihydrate crystals, the reaction slurry must be cooled. This cooling is achieved in the Low-Level Flash
     Cooler.

     This vessel is maintained under vacuum by the LLFC Vacuum Pump. The reaction slurry is circulated through the vessel
     by the LLFC Circulation Pump, the return being by gravity to the head of the reaction system.

     The evaporation of water from the slurry causes a cooling effect in the slurry and due to the very high recirculation rate,
     the delta T is maintained below 3°C. Supersaturation and scaling are therefore reduced in the flash cooler and piping. A
     delta T (between the inlet and the outlet legs) provides the evaporative load.

     The high recirculation rate inside the reactor ensured by the LLFC Circulation Pump also provides acidity and sulfate
     excess to the head of the reaction system where the phosphate rock is fed, essential for optimal dissolution and
     crystallization.

     The vapours released from the LLFC pass first through the Precondenser (where a part of the heat contained in the
     vapours is recovered to heat the water bleed coming from the Fluorine Scrubber that will finally be used as gypsum cake
     washing water onto the filters.

     3.2.2            Digestion Section

     The slurry from the last compartment of the attack reactor overflows by gravity to the Digestion tank which comprises
     three compartments.

     The residence time inside the Digestion Tank ensures that crystallisation of the gypsum and fluosilicates is completed
     before filtration. It provides additional time to finalise crystal growth and reduce super-saturation. The reduction of
     super-saturation means that a maturated slurry is fed to the gypsum filter reducing the scaling effect downstream and
     reducing the post-precipitation in the weak acid storage tank.

     In addition, the attack and digestion configuration ensure the prevention of short-circuiting of unreacted rock from the
     reaction system to the filtration.


     3.3              Gas Scrubbing

     A gas scrubber is installed for the reaction and filtration sections.

     The scrubbing system consists of an empty – counter-current Gas Scrubbing Tower with a Gas Scrubber Fan sucking and
     cleaning gases from the various emission points of the attack, digestion and filtration sections and discharging them to
     the Gas Stack.

     The gases go into the gas scrubbing tower through the bottom. They are washed two times with a circulating water
     solution. The water solution circulates through the circulation pumps. Water make-ups are done with process water for
     the second stage and pond water for the first stage.


     3.4              Filtration

     The gypsum slurry is fed to two Filtration trains in parallel, each fitted with a vacuum box and one external Filter
     Separator.

     Any acid droplets recovered by these separators are routed to the recycled acid pumps, The vapours from the Filter
     Separator pass through a Filter Condenser where they are condensed using cooling water. The remaining gases are going
     to the Filter Vacuum Pump. The water leaving the Filter Condensers in addition with the water coming from the Attack
     Condenser are routed to the Gypsum Cake Hoppers and re-used for the gypsum cake re-slurrying.

     collected in the Filter Cake Wash Tank.

     3.6             Fluorine Absorption

     In each concentration line, a system for fluorine absorption is provided. Vapors liberated from the acid contain principally
     water, HF, SiF4. To protect the environment, most of the fluorine content must be recovered.

     In order to ensure that the fluorosilicic acid produced is not contaminated with phosphoric acid, the vapours initially pass
     through a high efficiency Droplet Separator where the small droplets of entrained phosphoric acid are separated and
     returned to the concentration unit.

     One of the principal reasons for low recovery of H2SiF6 in traditional systems is the entrainment of fine droplets of acid.
     To prevent such an occurrence, a high efficiency Fluorosilicic Acid Droplets Separators similar to the ones after the
     Evaporators outlet is provided. The recovered acid droplets drain to the Fluorine Scrubber Seal tanks.

     weak acid to the two Weak Acid Settling Tanks. These tanks are fitted with a rake and a central collecting cone to recycle
     the weak acid sludge back to the reactor, with the Weak Acid Sludge. A small part of the sludge flow can also be recycled
     to the Weak Acid Distribution Box in order to promote the crystallization and settling.

     A small part of the sludge flow can also be directed to the Concentrated Acid Maturation Tank in order to promote the
     crystallization and settling.


     3.7.3             Fluorosilicic Acid (FSA)

     The Fluorine Scrubbing Acid (FSA), which contains 18% H₂SiF₆ (hexafluorosilicic acid), is produced in the fluorine
     recovery units during the process of capturing and neutralizing fluorine emissions. After the FSA is produced, it is
     discharged from the fluorine recovery units and directed into the FSA Storage Tank. This tank is specifically designed to
     hold the FSA safely until it is ready for further processing.

     Once the FSA is stored, it is subsequently transferred to the FSA Neutralization/Recovery Section. In this section, the FSA
     undergoes a neutralization process, where it is reacted with an appropriate neutralizing agent (such as lime or sodium
     carbonate) to reduce its acidity and transform the hexafluorosilicic acid into a less hazardous form. This process may also
     involve the recovery of valuable by-products such as calcium fluoride (CaF₂) or sodium fluoride (NaF), depending on the
     specific process used.


     3.8              Cooling Tower

     A cooling tower provides cold water to the concentration and filter condensers. The water is circulating in a closed loop
     on the concentration condensers.

     To ensure a low fluorine content in the cooling water, a make-up of process water is required. This make-up is performed
     feeding process water directly to the cooling tower and reusing process water coming from the liquid ring of the
     concentration vacuum pumps.
